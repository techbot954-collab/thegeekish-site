Starlink V3 turns the satellite internet race into a launch cadence problem

The next Starlink upgrade is not just about satellites. It is a reminder that low Earth orbit broadband is won by payloads, rockets, and boringly reliable schedules.

NB
Updated September 28, 2026

Concise Geekish brief based on reporting from The Verge and Amazon Leo mission updates.

What happened

The Verge reports that SpaceX is preparing to deploy its first Starlink V3 satellites on a Starship flight, with 26 upgraded satellites riding on the mission.

The same report frames the launch against Amazon Leo, Amazon's low Earth orbit internet network formerly known as Project Kuiper. Amazon says Leo has completed 14 missions and launched more than 375 satellites, while The Verge cites 396 launched satellites after the July 2 Atlas V mission.

Why the gap matters

Satellite broadband sounds like a space story, but the competitive edge is partly a logistics story. The Verge says Amazon's last successful Leo deployment was July 2, while launch-partner problems have slowed its next steps.

Amazon's own mission page says the company has more than 100 launches secured and plans to increase launch rate over time. That is the useful signal: the network only becomes real for customers when enough hardware repeatedly reaches orbit.

Quick takeaways

  • SpaceX's V3 Starlink push makes Starship part of the satellite internet story, not just a rocket spectacle.
  • Amazon Leo has launched hundreds of satellites, but still trails Starlink's operating scale by a wide margin, according to The Verge.
  • The next phase is less about announcements and more about whether launch cadence can become routine.
